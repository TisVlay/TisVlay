Good moment for this — the receipts are all in the branch history, so I can answer from evidence rather than vibes.

## The outcome vs. the exploration goal

The methodology paid off more than any single technology choice. Three decisions made the exploration cheap relative to its payoff: leaning fully into each candidate's idioms instead of building a comparison abstraction (a shared wire trait would have hidden exactly the things that mattered — you can't discover the consume-only-peer-leaf law through an abstraction layer), betting that the hardest-won code had zero D-Bus in it (held completely — the LG transport, CEC identity model, WoL, pairing semantics ported essentially verbatim, so the spike only re-derived the *plumbing*, not the domain knowledge), and making battle-testing the exit criterion rather than compile-parity. That last one is where the rewrite was actually earned: the three QA rounds caught a whole class of port-simplification bugs — global carrier events, the `/proc/net` ARP parse, lock-wake existing in config but having no generator, verify-retry tuning wired to nothing. The recurring lesson worth keeping: **a port preserves semantics or it preserves nothing; matching the shape is how you silently drop the load-bearing part.**

On the honest ledger: zenoh's cost is precisely the nine-item empirical-laws list in architecture.md. Every one of those was a debugging session, not a documentation read — D-Bus's semantics were boring but written down; zenoh's sharp edges were *discovered*. We also paid real complexity for session isolation (two sockets, two sessions per client, the gateway design) to defeat transitive routing, and accepted an authorization regression to socket-group granularity. What we bought: liveliness turned out to be a genuinely better presence primitive than `NameOwnerChanged` (one mechanism drives the footer, About pane, CLI status, and start-order freedom), and cached publishers plus bevy change detection collapsed the entire state-forwarder layer into `publish_changed<T>` — that's the single most elegant seam in the codebase. And bevy_ecs was the quiet co-winner: every QA fix this week landed as "add a component, add a system" with an obvious seam (`PairVerifying`, `MonitoredInterface`, `TransportTraits`), and the world tests exercise invariants without a socket in sight. Its one recurring tax is the same-batch/deferred-Commands hazard, which we've now hit twice and named.

## How the other two candidates would have fared

**zlink/Varlink** would have inverted our difficulty map. The places zenoh fought us would have been free: Varlink is point-to-point over per-service sockets, so the entire transitive-routing problem — the gateway design, the two-session clients, the serve-side law — simply doesn't exist; user-key isolation would have been the filesystem, day one. Typed error replies are native to the protocol (our `WireError`-as-JSON-reply-payload encoding is a convention we imposed). Streaming discovery maps directly onto a `more`-flagged call. But the places zenoh carried us would have been hand-written: Varlink has no pub/sub, no cache, no third-party-observable presence. The state fan-out — hydrate-then-live mirrors for GUI/tray/CLI, which is *most of what this application does* — would mean building per-client subscription streams with server-side replay ourselves, and presence would only exist between connected pairs (the GUI observing the tray's aliveness requires the tray to serve it, not a token in a shared space). My estimate: parity reachable, roughly comparable total effort, but the complexity would live in code we own instead of laws we learned — plus the maturity risk that made us sequence it second was real; we shipped on zenoh before zlink finished construction.

**tokio-UDS DIY** would have been slower to parity and I think we'd have regretted it precisely *because* of what the zenoh spike taught us: the feature list we actually leaned on — hydration-with-history, liveliness, multi-reply queries, reconnect semantics — is a list of mini-projects in DIY form, and each one would have had its own discovered bugs, except discovered in our protocol instead of in a framework with upstream eyes on it. Its genuine advantages (total transparency, no `unstable` feature flag, trivially sandbox-friendly, smallest dep tree) are the mirror image of zenoh's opacity. As a control group it did its job by never being needed: neither framework disappointed enough to trigger it.

The honest caveat over all of this: at two TVs, six processes, one machine, *all three would have worked*. The differentiators that decided it — the data-space model fitting a state-fan-out application, and the cross-host future for free — are one structural fit and one option on the future. If the G4/multi-room story ever grows a second machine, the zenoh choice cashes its remaining check; until then, the thing that made this rewrite good wasn't picking the winner, it was the discipline around how we picked and the refusal to call it done before it survived real hardware.

A follow-up appended after the first post-parity QA weekend — the wire-v2 rework, the reliable-screen/primary-input plan, and roughly a dozen field-found defects later:

## What the soak taught that parity couldn't

The exit interview above was written at compile-and-battle-test parity; the two days since tested a different claim — how the architecture behaves under sustained field QA on the packaged stack, where every round is an `nh os switch` against the living room C3 rather than a fixture. The headline: **the port-preserves-semantics lesson recurred twice more, exactly as predicted, in places the parity rounds couldn't reach.** Root's reliable-screen elevation — the pre-flight wake that made `screen_on` mean "and wake the TV if you must" — had been silently dropped while its sibling (CEC screen-via-power emulation) survived, so the port *looked* complete; only unlocking a desktop against a powered-off TV exposed the missing half. And `automate_when` was the same failure in mirror image: the spike grew a per-TV field during the meta port, the profile-level field kept resolving through the whole inheritance chain, and nothing read the result — two compiling surfaces, one dead, undetectable by any test that didn't ask which one the gate consumed. Root parity restored in both cases meant deleting spike-grown structure, not adding it.

On the zenoh ledger, the most satisfying entry since: the no-reply law — the ugliest of the nine empirical laws, previously compensated with retry loops — *dissolved* instead of getting another workaround. Zenoh's `Querier::matching_status` turned out to be the readiness primitive the retries were approximating, and once probe-validated on both production topologies it collapsed four hand-rolled rpc copies into one matching-gated `Caller`. The transferable lesson: when you find yourself writing a compensation loop against a framework, first check whether the framework has the primitive you're reimplementing — the laws list shrank for the first time instead of growing.

The bevy_ecs claim from above — "every fix lands as a component and a system" — held under a heavier load than the original QA rounds: the surviving-teardown screen latch is one flag on an existing component, primary-input restore is one marker plus one drain system, and each shipped with a world test that pins the regression without a socket in sight. The counterpart discovery on the GUI side was less flattering: Slint's std `CheckBox` severs its declarative binding on first user toggle, which argues for the same philosophy in the UI layer that the world already follows — render state from the model, never let a widget own it.

Two process notes earned their keep. First, the plan-file-plus-adversarial-review workflow (three parallel reviewers before implementation) caught genuine blockers at design time that would have been expensive later: the session-unlock rpc being byte-identical to a manual click (the automation flag had to become resolved intent on the wire), a second screen latch that would have raced the first, and `ScreenOff` sneaking into the wake set where it would have regressed the lock-screen path root had explicitly optimized. Second, field QA kept finding what no simulation could: LG rejecting in-band `power_on` surfaced as one journal line, the auto-login lock arriving before the watcher existed as an edge nobody ever saw, the TV recalling its last input on WoL confirming a design premise. The C3 remains the only test rig that matters; everything else is rehearsal.

Where it stands: the wake/screen/primary-input plan is implemented end to end — elevation, pre-flight wake routed by transport, one surviving verify latch, per-event `restore_input` policy riding the wire as resolved intent, idle drift re-assert, profile-chain `automate_when`, schema version tags — soaking with PR #106 through the duty week. The rewrite is still smaller than what it replaced, and the gap between "ported" and "faithful" is now a category we test for rather than a surprise we keep re-learning.
